How to write a web page

Add this file to your Claude project with the others. It is the same for every brand.

An email arrives at somebody. A page waits for them. They arrive sceptical, in a hurry, from somewhere you did not choose, and they leave at the first moment nothing is holding them.

Instructions for Claude

The top of the page is the whole job

You have about five seconds before somebody decides whether to keep reading. In those five seconds they are silently asking five things:

  1. What is this?
  2. Is it for me?
  3. Why should I care?
  4. Why you and not the others?
  5. Do I believe you?

The headline and the line under it have to answer the first three. The proof beside them answers the fifth. If the top of the page cannot do that, nothing below it gets read.

Scoring a headline

Five things, and two of them count double, because those two are where homepage lines actually fail.

WeightThe question
Clarity1One read. Do you know what it is and who it is for?
Pull2Does it lead with what the reader wants, rather than what the thing is?
Difference2Can you hear "unlike the others"? Could only this company have written it?
Specificity1Concrete, or vague adjectives?
Voice1Plain and human, passes the slop rules?

Score each out of five, double the two in bold, and give the reason beside every score. Above about 21 out of 25 it is ready. Below 15 it needs starting again rather than editing.

⭐ Score each line cold, against the questions, never against the last one you wrote. A weak set makes a mediocre line look good.

What the rest of the page is for

Three jobs, in this order, because it is the order people think in.

Trust, at the top. Proof goes beside the headline, not further down. Waiting to earn trust later means earning it from somebody who has already gone. Logos, ratings, a number, a named customer.

Belief, through the middle. This is where you make them want it and show them it works.

  • How it works. Three steps, scannable in a couple of seconds. If the process looks like effort, people leave. The point is to answer "how hard is this going to be" before they ask it.
  • What they get out of it. Outcomes, not features. "Built to last ten years, tested across five thousand uses" rather than "high quality materials". Specific is what makes it believed.
  • What is included. Plainly. Uncertainty does not convert.
  • Proof, layered. Not one testimonial in a box. Written reviews with real names, ratings, video if it exists. Video is harder to fake and people know that.

Decision, at the bottom. They are close, and now they are looking for reasons not to.

  • How you compare. Side by side with the alternatives, honestly. Brands avoid this and it is usually a mistake.
  • The questions they are actually worried about. Five to eight, and every one is a doubt you are removing rather than filler. Price, what happens if it goes wrong, whether it works for their situation, how to get out of it.
  • The last call, which should feel different from the one at the top. The top one is functional. This one speaks to the outcome.

Rules that matter more on a page than anywhere else

One primary action. A page that asks for three things gets none of them. Secondary links can exist, but only one thing looks like the button.

Put the action where intent peaks, not just at the end. People decide at different points. A single call to action at the bottom only catches the ones who read everything, and most do not.

Write for the skimmer as well as the reader. Roughly seven in ten scan. Headings and the first line of each section have to carry the argument on their own. Read only the headings back and see whether the case still stands up.

Specific beats impressive. A number, a date, a name. "Ten years" outperforms "long lasting" every time, and it is easier to write.

Say what it costs, or say why you cannot. A page that hides the price teaches people you are expensive and evasive.

Before you show it

  • The five-second questions answered above the fold.
  • The headline scored, with reasons, and the score shown.
  • One primary action, repeated where intent peaks.
  • The headings alone still make the case.
  • Every claim traceable to the brand's proof file.
  • Slop rules run, both passes.
  • Claims ledger shown.
  • Scored against the messaging framework, honestly.
