Training guide / for Thomas

Build your own website with Claude Code

You are going to build a website by talking to your laptop. Not a template, not a page builder. A real Next.js site, every pixel yours, deployed on Vercel, that you change by saying what you want out loud.

The hard part is not the code. Claude Code writes the code, and it writes it well.

The hard part is that an agent with no constraints will happily make you a different website every time you ask it for a page. Rounded corners here, a gradient there, a headline in Title Case because it felt right in the moment. Ten pages in, you have ten designs and no brand.

So this guide front-loads the boring bit. Before you write a single line of code you build two documents: a brand spec and a messaging framework. They are the contract. Once they exist, every new page is a lookup rather than a negotiation. That is the whole trick, and it is the reason this works when it works.

How to read this

Nine phases, in order. Phases 1 to 3 contain no code at all. Do not skip them. They are the ones that decide whether you have a website or a pile of pages.

Two artefacts

This page, and the kit

There are two things here, and they are for two different readers.

This page is for you. It explains why each phase exists and what a good decision looks like. Read it once, end to end, before you start.

Set up voice properly, it matters more than you think

You are about to dictate long, precise instructions for weeks. Get this right now and the build is a pleasure. Get it wrong and you will fight it the whole way.

  1. Pick one push-to-talk key and never change it. Hold, speak, release. Do not use always-on listening, or you will eventually dictate half a phone call into your terminal.
  2. Add a custom vocabulary if your tool supports it. Feed it the words it will mangle: Next.js, Vercel, Tailwind, npm, repo, hex, viewport, and your own brand and product names. Whisper models turn "Vercel" into "Verso" and "Versailles" with real enthusiasm.
  3. Read what it typed before you hit enter. Every time. A transcription slip in a hex code is a silent bug. A slip in a filename is a file you did not mean to touch.
  4. Speak in whole thoughts. Voice rewards this. One paragraph beats five short commands.
The one habit that changes everything

Describe the outcome, not the code. Say "the nav feels cramped on mobile", not "add horizontal padding of 6 to the nav". You are the client and the art director. Let it be the builder. It is better at being a builder than you are, and you are better at knowing what looks right.

Phase 1 / no code

Find the aesthetic

You do not have a design. Good. You are not going to invent one from nothing and you are not going to pick a template. You are going to steal an aesthetic and make it yours, which is what every designer who has ever lived actually does.

Collect the evidence

Go and find ten to fifteen websites you genuinely admire. Not competitors. Anything at all. A furniture shop, a magazine, a bank, a Japanese stationery brand. Range is good, because the pattern that survives across very different sites is the pattern that is actually yours.

For each one, take these screengrabs. On a Mac, Cmd + Shift + 4, then drag a box.

  1. The full homepage, top to bottom. Use a full-page capture if you can, or three or four grabs down the page.
  2. One section you love, close in. The hero. A card grid. The footer.
  3. The typography, tight. Zoom the browser to 150% and grab a heading with a paragraph under it, so the relationship between the two sizes is visible. That relationship is most of what you are responding to.
  4. A button. Seriously. Buttons carry far more brand than people realise.
  5. The mobile view. In Chrome: right-click, Inspect, click the phone icon, choose an iPhone, grab it.

All of them into one folder, named so you can talk about them: 01-kinfolk-homepage.png, 01-kinfolk-type.png, 02-monzo-buttons.png.

Write down why, before Claude tells you

Two lines per site in a text file next to the images. What you like, what you hate. Do this before you show Claude anything, because the moment it starts describing them back to you it will anchor you and you will quietly lose your own opinion.

Now hand them over

Say this

"Look at every image in this folder. For each site, tell me what makes it feel the way it does. Be specific and physical: how much white space, how big is the type, how many colours are actually on the page, are the corners sharp or round, is there any shadow, does anything move. Then find the pattern across all of them and tell me what I seem to be drawn to. Do not be polite about it. If my taste is inconsistent, say so and show me where."

That last sentence earns its place. You want the contradictions surfaced now, not in Phase 6.

Then push it:

Then say this

"Give me three different directions I could take, each one a paragraph, each one a real choice with a real trade-off. Do not give me a safe middle option."

Pick one. Or take pieces from each. But decide, and say out loud what you decided and why. That sentence becomes the first line of your brand spec.

The one rule for this phase

Do not let it copy a site. You are extracting a feeling, not cloning a layout. Ask for "a homepage like Stripe's" and you will get a bad Stripe. Ask for "the calm, generous white space and the one-accent-colour discipline that Stripe has, applied to my brand", and you will get yours.

Phase 2 / no code

Write the brand spec

This is the most important document in the project.

A brand spec is not a mood board. It is a contract an agent cannot argue with. Every rule in it has to be mechanically checkable. If a rule cannot be checked, it is not a rule, it is a vibe, and a vibe will not survive contact with page four.

The test for every single line

Could a script check this?

"Modern and clean." Not a rule. Means nothing. Delete it.
"Approachable but professional." Same. Delete it.
"The accent colour is #3A7CA5 and it is the only saturated colour on the page."
"No border-radius anywhere. Corners are sharp."
"Body text is 16px, weight 300, line-height 1.7."
"Transitions are 0.3s ease, and only on transform and opacity."
"Headings are sentence case. Never Title Case."

Numbers, hex codes, and flat bans. That is the entire vocabulary.

The ten sections

Ask Claude to draft each one from your screengrabs and your chosen direction, then argue with it until every line passes the test above.

#SectionWhat it must contain
1ColoursEvery colour as a hex code, with the CSS variable name and what it is for. Then the rules: which colour is the only loud one, what never goes on what, what is banned. Keep the palette small. Two neutrals, one accent, one dark. If you are at seven colours you have not made a decision.
2TypographyThe full scale as a table: hero, section heading, sub-heading, body, small, label. For each: family, size in px, weight, letter-spacing, where it is used. Then the rules. Which weights are allowed. Sentence case or not.
3LogoHow it is built, what colour, how much clear space, what you may never do to it.
4Layout & spacingContainer max-width. Section padding, desktop and mobile. A spacing scale you are allowed to use (8, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, 96) and a ban on any number outside it. How wide body copy may run.
5ComponentsButtons in every state, exact colours, exact type. Cards. Links. Form fields. Rendered as real examples, not descriptions.
6MotionDurations, easing, and what is allowed to animate: transform and opacity, essentially nothing else, because everything else makes the page stutter. Three or four moments of motion per page. Not more. Must respect prefers-reduced-motion, always.
7ImageryWhat kind, what treatment, what is banned. "No stock photography" is a real and excellent rule.
8ResponsiveYour breakpoints, with the exact padding and type changes at each one.
9VoiceNot adjectives. Examples. Five real sentences you would be happy to publish, five you would not, and why.
10Never do thisA flat list of bans. Fifteen to twenty of them.

Section 10 does most of the work

Write it last, once you know what you hate. It will look something like this:

Rounded corners
Gradients
Drop shadows
Pure black or pure white
Title Case headings
Em dashes
Stock photography
Emoji as decoration
Decorative icons
Text on a photo with no scrim
A second accent colour
Content hidden behind hover
Animating layout properties
Ignoring prefers-reduced-motion
Why this section matters

A rule phrased as a ban is a rule an agent cannot negotiate with. "Keep it clean" is an invitation to interpret. "No gradients" is not.

Say this

"Turn what we agreed into a brand spec. Every rule must be something a script could check: a hex code, a number, or a flat ban. If you write a line I could not check, delete it and replace it with one I could. Use these ten sections: colours, typography, logo, layout and spacing, components, motion, imagery, responsive, voice, never-do-this. End with at least fifteen flat prohibitions."

Phase 3 / no code

Write the messaging framework

Paul will do this one with you, and it is worth an hour of his time. Here is what it is, so you turn up ready.

A messaging framework stops you renegotiating what the whole thing is every time you write a headline. Same principle as the brand spec, applied to words instead of pixels.

Five sections, top down. Each feeds the next, so do not skip ahead.

1. Core value proposition

  • The one line. It has to survive "so what?"
  • Functional benefit. Concretely, what it does.
  • Emotional benefit. What internal state it resolves.
  • Social benefit. What choosing you says about the person choosing. Skip it if it is not really true. A forced one is worse than none.

2. Positioning

The strategic anchor. Everything downstream hangs off this one.

  • Category. Be precise. "HRIS technology consulting", not "consulting".
  • Target audience. A role, in a situation, with a tension, that creates a need. Not a demographic.
  • What makes you different. One thing. Not a feature list. If you have three, you have none.
  • The statement, filled in: "For [audience] who [tension], [brand] is the [category] that [key benefit] because [proof]."

3. Messaging pillars

Three or four. Each a genuinely different dimension. If two overlap, merge them. For each pillar:

  • Name. Short, specific, memorable. "Quality service" is not a pillar.
  • Claim. The belief you want the market to accept.
  • Proof points. Facts, numbers, named things. Real ones. Never invent a proof point. If you have not got one, the pillar is not ready.
  • Competitive contrast. What others do worse, specifically.

4. Messaging hierarchy

Most important message to least.

  • Hero message. The billboard.
  • Primary headline. The rational backing underneath it.
  • Approved lines. A small bank of exact phrases, pre-cleared, so you are not rewriting your own boilerplate at midnight.

5. Voice and tone

  • One sentence, using a metaphor. "Like a smart colleague sharing war stories over coffee."
  • Four to six writing principles as "X, not Y" pairs. The contrast is what makes them usable.
  • A never-use list. Actual banned words.
The rule that makes it operational

When you ask Claude for copy, it must cite which pillar and which proof point it used. Not "theme around" the framework. Cite it.

"Before you write, tell me which pillar this page leads with and which proof point backs it. Then write. Then list which framework elements you used."

Without that, a pillar quietly decays into a loose label within a fortnight and you are back to making it up as you go.

Phase 5 / the important one

Wire the brand in so it cannot be ignored

This is where most people go wrong. They write a beautiful brand document, put it in a folder, and then wonder why page four has rounded corners.

Read this twice

A rule in a document is not a gate. It is a note. A gate is something that blocks. If a failure matters, it needs a check that stops the work.

You need four layers. Each one exists for a different reader.

Layer 1. CLAUDE.md at the root of the repo

Claude Code reads this automatically at the start of every session in that folder, forever. It is the always-on law. Keep it short and terse. This is not the place for the full spec, it is the place for the things that must never be forgotten.

# my-site

Next.js + Tailwind. Deployed on Vercel from `main`.

## Brand (law)
- Accent: #3A7CA5 (--accent). The only saturated colour on any page.
- Dark:   #1A3A4E (--dark). Not black. Never use #000.
- Background: #FAFAF8 (--bg). Never pure white.
- Text:  #1D1B1B (--text).
- Fonts: JetBrains Mono (default), Space Grotesk (display only).
- Full spec: docs/BRAND.md. Read it before building any page.
- Messaging: docs/MESSAGING.md. Read it before writing any copy.

## Never
- No rounded corners. No gradients. No box-shadows.
- No Title Case. Sentence case everywhere.
- No em dashes.
- No stock photography.
- Never animate anything except transform and opacity.
- Never commit to main without running `npm run build` first.

Layer 2. The tokens, in real CSS

The actual values in globals.css as CSS custom properties, mirrored into your Tailwind config. This is ground truth. If a colour is not in here, it does not exist.

:root {
  --bg:     #FAFAF8;
  --text:   #1D1B1B;
  --accent: #3A7CA5;
  --dark:   #1A3A4E;
  --muted:  #8A8A85;
}

Layer 3. The full spec at docs/BRAND.md

Your Phase 2 document, in full, in the repo. CLAUDE.md points at it. This is the file that answers "what exactly is the accent colour used for". Put the messaging framework beside it at docs/MESSAGING.md.

One source of truth

When you later write helper prompts or skills, they must point at this file, never copy it. The moment you have two copies of your brand spec they begin drifting apart, and you will not notice until the day they contradict each other.

Layer 4. The audit gate, which is the one that actually saves you

This is the step that separates a site which stays on-brand from one that slowly rots. Make it check its own work before it shows you anything. Add this to CLAUDE.md:

## Before showing me any new or changed page, you MUST:
1. Run `npm run build`. It must pass.
2. Grep the file for every banned pattern and paste the results:
   - `rounded`, `border-radius`  -> must be zero hits
   - `gradient`                  -> must be zero hits
   - `shadow`                    -> must be zero hits
   - hex codes                   -> every one must be in the palette above
3. Screenshot the page at 1440px and at 390px, and LOOK at both.
4. Show me a table: check | what I grepped | expected | found | pass/fail.
Do not tell me it is done until every row passes.

Blunt, mechanical, and it works. An agent cannot mark its own homework "looks good to me" if you have told it exactly what to grep for.

Layer 5, optional, once you are shipping regularly

A brand.json holding your palette, your allowed fonts and a CSS never-list (maxBorderRadiusPx: 0, noGradient: true, noBoxShadow: true), plus a small script that measures a rendered screenshot against it. Overkill on day one. Worth it by page twenty.

Phase 6

Build the pages

Now it is fun, and it is fast.

Build the design system before the pages

Do not start with the homepage. Start with the alphabet.

Say this

"Build me a page at /styleguide that renders every element in docs/BRAND.md: the full type scale, every colour swatch with its hex and variable name, every button state, the card, the form fields. Live rendered, not described. This page is for me to check the system, it will not be public."

Look at it. Fix it here. Everything downstream inherits from it, so an hour spent now saves ten later. This page is also your future public /brand page, the human-readable mirror of the spec. Keep it.

Then the pages, one at a time

Wireframe in words first, always:

Say this

"Before you write any code, describe the about page to me section by section. What is in each one, what does it do, why is it in that order. Do not code yet."

Argue with the structure. Then let it build. Structure first, then prose, and the approved structure never drifts.

The loop, once it is on screen

This is where you will spend most of your time, and it is all voice.

Things you will say a lot

"The gap between the heading and the paragraph is too tight."

"That accent colour is doing too much work on this page. Pull it back to just the links."

"This section is fine but it is boring. Give me three other ways to lay it out and show me all three."

"It looks wrong on my phone. Screenshot it at 390 wide and tell me what is broken."

The most useful sentence in this guide

"Screenshot it and look at it." Make it show you, not tell you. An agent will happily describe a beautiful page that it has never actually looked at.

And when it goes sideways

It will. A change will break something else. When it does, do not patch forward.

git diff              # what did it actually change
git checkout .        # throw it away, start that instruction again

This is why you commit constantly. A bad ten minutes should cost you ten minutes.

Phase 7

Deploy to Vercel

The good news: about four minutes.

  1. Go to vercel.com, log in with GitHub.
  2. Add New → Project. It lists your repos. Pick my-site.
  3. It detects Next.js on its own. Change nothing. Click Deploy.
  4. A minute later you have a live URL like my-site-xyz.vercel.app. That is your site, on the internet.

What just happened, and why it is the good bit

Vercel is now watching your GitHub repo. From here on:

git add -A
git commit -m "New about page"
git push

and it is live in about a minute. That is your entire deploy process, forever. "Commit" now means "publish". Get comfortable with that.

Your domain

In Vercel: Project → Settings → Domains → Add. Type your domain. Vercel tells you exactly which DNS records to create. Go to wherever you bought the domain, paste them in, wait. Usually minutes, occasionally a couple of hours. Vercel handles the SSL certificate itself, so you get HTTPS without lifting a finger.

The two things that will bite you

Remember these

1. Vercel only deploys main. If you are working on a branch, your changes are not live no matter how many times you commit.

2. It builds on Vercel's machine, not yours. A page that works locally can still fail the deploy. This is exactly why npm run build before every push is written into your CLAUDE.md. If it builds locally, it will build there.

Preview deploys, free and excellent

Push a branch instead of main and Vercel builds a private URL for that branch alone. Send it to Paul. He looks, he comments, and nothing on the live site has moved. Merge to main when you are happy.

Phase 8

Living with it

The site is live. Now it is a thing you own and change constantly. Four habits that decide whether this is a joy or a chore.

Orient it at the start of every session

Claude Code reads CLAUDE.md automatically, but say it anyway: "Read docs/BRAND.md and docs/MESSAGING.md before you touch anything today."

Keep CLAUDE.md alive

Every time you catch yourself correcting the same thing twice, that correction is a missing rule. Add it. "Add that to CLAUDE.md as a permanent rule so I never have to say it again." That is how the file earns its keep, and how the site gets easier to build rather than harder.

One job per session

Do not do the pricing page and the nav and the blog in one conversation. Long conversations drift, and so does the work in them.

Never let it say "done" without evidence

Not "I've updated the page." Show me the build passing, show me the screenshot, show me the audit table. Reading a diff and finding it convincing is not proof that a page works.

Phase 9

The chatbot

Once the site is live and stable. It is genuinely not hard. Three pieces.

1. An API key

From console.anthropic.com. Put it in a file called .env.local at the root of the project:

ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=sk-ant-...
Do not get this wrong

Check .env.local is in your .gitignore. With your own eyes. A key pushed to GitHub is compromised within minutes, and deleting it afterwards does not help, because it is already in the history and already scraped. Never paste a key into a document, a chat, or a page.

Add it in Vercel → Settings → Environment Variables too, or it will work on your laptop and break in production.

2. A server route

At src/app/api/chat/route.ts. It takes the visitor's message, calls the Claude API, returns the answer. The key stays on the server and never reaches the browser.

3. A chat widget, in your brand

Not a bubble in somebody else's colours. Yours. It obeys docs/BRAND.md like every other component.

The bit that actually matters: what it knows

A chatbot that knows nothing is a toy. A chatbot that makes things up is a liability.

  • Give it a system prompt built from your messaging framework. It should sound like the brand because it has been handed the brand.
  • Give it only your real content. Your pages, your positions, your facts. Nothing else.
  • Tell it explicitly what to do when it does not know. "If you do not know, say so and offer to put them in touch. Never guess a figure, a date, a position or a commitment."

That last rule is not optional. An agent will invent a plausible-sounding answer if you do not forbid it, and it will do so in a warm, confident, entirely convincing tone. On a public-facing site, a made-up answer is a quote you did not say.

Say this

"Build me a chat widget in the site's brand, with an API route that calls Claude. The system prompt should be built from docs/MESSAGING.md and the real content of my pages, and nothing else. It must refuse to invent figures, dates, positions or commitments, and should offer to put the person in touch with me instead. Keep the key server-side only, and check it is gitignored."

Reference

The short version

Pin this somewhere.

  1. Screengrabs before decisions. Steal an aesthetic, do not clone a site.
  2. A rule that cannot be checked is not a rule. Hex codes, numbers, flat bans.
  3. A rule in a doc is not a gate. Put it in CLAUDE.md and make it grep for its own violations before it shows you anything.
  4. One source of truth. Point at the spec. Never copy it.
  5. Structure before prose. Wireframe in words, argue, then build.
  6. Make it show you, not tell you. Screenshot it and look at it.
  7. Commit constantly. It is your undo button and it is free.
  8. Describe the outcome, not the code. You are the art director, not the builder.
  9. Correcting the same thing twice means a rule is missing. Go and write it.
