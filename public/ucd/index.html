How it's done

Slow, then fast

Every one of those works the same way underneath. You get clear on what good looks like first, slowly, once. Then you give the tool three things so it hits that standard every time. After that it's fast, and you're not starting from a blank chat every time you sit down.

A clear job

One thing you want it to do, said plainly. Not a vague question.

The stuff it needs

Give it what you'd give a colleague to do the job: the notes, the data, the examples.

What good looks like

Show it a good result, so it has a standard to hit, not a guess to make.

The one worth having

How to get good research from AI

Good research from AI isn't a magic prompt, it's a process. Brief it properly, run it more than once, and make the models check each other. Specs, not tricks.

1
Write a bad prompt
The rough one-liner. Don't polish it.
2
Get the AI to write the brief
It turns the rough ask into a proper one: the goal, a good and a bad example, the sources that count, what it's for.
3
Run it in all three research modes
Not a plain chat. The mode that actually reads live sources.
Claude researchChatGPT researchGemini research
4
Make them mark each other's work
Give each model the other two reports. Critique and build.
5
Then a fresh, hostile pass
A clean model: find the gaps, verify the claims, challenge everything, say what's missing.
6
A strong starting point, not the truth
You decide what's credible and what it means.

Here it is run for real, on a question a team like yours might ask.

1 · The bad prompt
What does the research say about AI tutoring?
2 · The brief it writes back
Role. Act as a senior education-technology analyst briefing the leadership of a university executive-education group. You are numerate, sceptical of vendor and press claims, and you separate peer-reviewed evidence from marketing.
The decision this feeds. We're deciding whether and how to build AI tutoring and AI-assisted feedback into our executive programmes over the next two years. I need a clear-eyed read of what the evidence supports, not a hype piece and not a doom piece.
Core question. What does the current evidence say about whether AI tutoring and AI-assisted feedback improve adult and executive learning outcomes, where it works, where it doesn't, and what the real risks are?
Specifically cover. (1) The strongest studies on outcomes, with effect sizes and the populations studied, and how strong that evidence really is. (2) Where it helps most and least. (3) The known failure modes: over-reliance, deskilling, shallow engagement, accuracy. (4) What good implementations do differently from bad ones. (5) The gaps, especially for professional and executive learners.
Sources. Prioritise peer-reviewed studies, meta-analyses and named institutional research. Treat vendor case studies and "up to X%" press figures as claims to check, not facts. When a number comes from a vendor, say so.
Output. Open with a 150-word executive summary giving your actual read. Then a section per question above. End with a short "what I'd watch" list.
Honesty. Where the evidence is thin or conflicting, show both sides rather than averaging them. Flag your confidence on each major claim. If a widely repeated figure traces to a single unreplicated study, call it out. Note anything you couldn't verify.
3 · A slice of what comes back

The read. Well-built AI tutoring genuinely lifts learning, and the newest trials are striking, but the size of the gain depends on how the tutor is designed, and almost all the hard evidence is on students, not executives.

2× A Harvard randomised trial (194 students) found people learned more than twice as much, in less time, with a well-built AI tutor than in an active-learning class. Scientific Reports, 2025
0.66 A meta-analysis of 50 trials put the median effect at 0.66 standard deviations, 50th to 75th percentile, but far smaller on standardised tests than on local ones. Kulik & Fletcher, 2016
0.42 A larger meta-analysis (107 studies, 14,321 learners) found a more modest but real edge over ordinary class teaching. Ma et al., 2014

What's thin. Nearly all of this is school and university students. The evidence for executive and professional learners barely exists. That gap is the opportunity.

Sanity-check it Credible sources, not just easy ones? Does the cited source actually say it? Where's it thin?

What a system looks like

What "change my website" really sets off

I ask for one small change to my site in plain English. Behind that one line, a whole set of steps runs every time: checking it against the brand, building it, looking at it, and pushing it live. That is the system. You build it once, then every change goes through it.

Where this is going

It went from chatting, to making things, to building things.

Which changes the move. You stop opening a fresh chat every time and start building in systems that hold your context, your examples and your standard, so the good version comes back every time.

Putting it to work

Three questions for an agent

A simple, memorable spine. Where does it fit? What is it, exactly? How do we adopt it safely? Everything else hangs off these three.

1

Where does an agent fit?

Pick the job that's costing you: staff hours, or students falling through the cracks. A good candidate job is one that:

Happens all the time. Every student email, every application, every deadline.
Has a clear finish line. Query answered, form checked, reminder sent, flag raised.
Touches systems you already use. Email, Moodle or Canvas, student records, calendars, spreadsheets.
Has edge cases that are annoying but learnable. Not so rote a simple rule handles it, not so sensitive it needs pure human judgement.
Has someone who feels the loss. Staff drowning in admin, missed deadlines.

Jobs they'll recognise: answering repetitive student FAQs, triaging the programme inbox to the right office, chasing missing enrolment documents, first-draft feedback on formative (not graded) work, coordinating placement or event logistics, checking application completeness, deadline reminders.

2

What is an agent, really?

An agent isn't magic. It's seven decisions. Here they are for one running example, a programme query assistant.

The 7 partsFor a student-query assistant
1Trigger. What wakes it up?A student emails the programme inbox.
2Context. What must it know?The handbook, timetable, key dates, FAQs.
3Tools. What can it use?Search the handbook, look up the timetable, draft an email.
4Allowed actions. What can it do itself?Draft a reply, tag the query.
5Approval. Where does a human sign off?Staff approve every reply before it sends, at first.
6Escalation. When does it hand to a human?Anything about extenuating circumstances, welfare, appeals, grades.
7Success. What does good look like?Accurate answer, fast. Staff time saved. Nothing sensitive auto-handled.

If the room leaves understanding only this table, the talk has done its job.

3

How do you adopt one responsibly?

Think of it as a safety ladder. Start at the top rung, and only descend as trust is earned.

Draft and approve
Always start here
A human signs off every time.
Triage
It routes, it doesn't act.
Coordinator
It moves things along: reminders, booking, chasing info.
Bounded action
It does one defined thing under clear rules, like sending a standard deadline reminder or booking a room.

Start as a predictable workflow, earn autonomy over time. The wrapper is the accountability: logs of what it did and why, human approval, the ability to test before go-live, and an override always available.

Before you let an agent near real student queries, run it against 50 real past ones with known-good answers, and show where it's right, where it flags, and where it errs. That is also exactly what you'd show an ethics or validation committee.

You don't need to become a programmer. You need to be able to describe a job precisely enough that something else can do it, and to know where a human must stay in the loop.
Some things stay human: welfare, mental health, grades of record, appeals, and anything pastoral. Agents clear the admin around those, so staff have more time for them, not less.
