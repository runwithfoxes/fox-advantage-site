Measuring what works, and the evidence behind common advice

Checked against Klaviyo's own pages and the outside sources named below on 17 Oct 2026. Help Center dates are the "updated" dates on the articles as saved that day. One caution applies to the whole file, and it is our reading, not Klaviyo's: Klaviyo's email price rises with the number of active profiles and its texts are paid per credit, so it earns more when lists are bigger and more is sent. Weigh its advice on sending more with that in mind. (Source: Klaviyo pricing page, no date shown, checked 17 Oct 2026, https://www.klaviyo.com/pricing)

How does Klaviyo decide which email gets credit for a sale?

By default Klaviyo uses last-touch attribution: a sale is credited to the last message that was opened or clicked inside a window. The default windows for a new account are 5 days for email opens, 5 days for email clicks, 5 days for text clicks, 1 day for text opens and 12 hours for text deliveries. Open on day 1, click on day 2, buy on day 4, and the email gets the revenue. Buy on day 12 and it does not. (documented) (Source: Klaviyo Help Center, "Understanding Klaviyo message attribution", updated 18 Aug 2026, https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/1260804504250)

So Klaviyo's revenue figures are attributed revenue, meaning revenue that followed an email. They are not revenue the email caused. Someone who was about to buy anyway and clicked a flow email counts in full. Every Klaviyo revenue figure below carries this limit.

You can change the windows under Settings, then Attribution. Bot clicks (from security software and some inbox providers) are removed from attribution and reporting by default on new accounts. A separate switch removes Apple Mail Privacy Protection opens from attribution, though not from reporting. Klaviyo warns that a long open window biases results towards opens. Changes recalculate your history within about 36 hours. (documented) (Source: Klaviyo Help Center, "How to change your attribution model", updated 15 Aug 2026, https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/11118357030555)

Can I trust open rates?

Not on their own. Apple Mail Privacy Protection (MPP) lets Apple Mail users have images loaded in advance, whether or not they read the email. An open is recorded when a tiny tracking image loads, so these loads look like opens. Klaviyo marks open events with a property called Apple Privacy Open, true or false, available from 20 Nov 2021. When it is true, the open came from someone with the feature switched on, whether or not they actually opened the email. (documented) (Source: Klaviyo Help Center, "How to identify iOS15 Mail Privacy Protection opens", updated 18 Aug 2026, https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/4416791883163) In its article on reports, Klaviyo says that in report exports it "cannot distinguish between a subscriber open versus an MPP open". (documented) (Source: Klaviyo Help Center, "How to configure custom reports to track Apple Mail Privacy Protection (MPP) opens", updated 18 Aug 2026, https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/4416803987739)

How much it inflates opens varies. A Klaviyo blog post from around Sept/Oct 2021 (the page shows no date) said network-wide open rates were up roughly 15% since September 2021, with no sample given (Klaviyo's data), and that revenue attribution "may be inflated". (Source: Klaviyo blog, "How Klaviyo email marketing users prep for Apple iOS 15", no date shown (our notes put it at Sept/Oct 2021), https://www.klaviyo.com/blog/apple-ios15-klaviyo) Omeda, a software firm, reported its clients' total open rates went from 22.6% to 40.5% within six months (outside data, one platform's clients, 2022). (Source: Omeda, "The Impact of Apple's Mail Privacy Protection 6 Months Later", 2022, https://omeda.com/the-impact-of-apples-mail-privacy-protection-6-months-later)

The practical rule: judge by clicks and orders, and treat opens as a rough signal. Bots add noise to clicks too, which is why Klaviyo strips bot clicks by default.

How do I measure what email adds?

Compare people who got the email with similar people who did not. That is a holdout group. It answers the question attribution cannot: would they have bought anyway?

Klaviyo's built-in tool is the global holdout group. It requires at least 400,000 profiles in total, so most small brands cannot use it. You choose a share to hold out, about 5% is recommended, and Klaviyo recommends running it for 3 months. Those people get no campaign or flow emails, texts or push messages, apart from transactional mail and any flows you choose to let through. One holdout runs at a time, across all channels. The results show a marketing lift percentage and a "win probability", Klaviyo's measure of the probability that your marketing provides a meaningful improvement over the holdout group. Letting some flows through shrinks the measured lift. Klaviyo publishes no results from the tool. (documented) (Sources: Klaviyo Help Center, "Getting started with global holdout groups", updated 15 Aug 2026, https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/18138290642971; Klaviyo blog, "Global Holdout Groups", undated, https://www.klaviyo.com/blog/global-holdout-groups)

Below 400,000 profiles, practitioner Ben Zettler describes a manual version (practitioner view, no data): pick one program such as the abandoned checkout flow, randomly leave out 10% to 20% of eligible people, run it two to four weeks at least, and compare revenue per profile in the two groups. He adds that last-click credit "will almost always overstate" email for brands with strong demand and repeat customers. (Source: Ben Zettler, Ecommerce Fastlane, "How To Measure Email & SMS Incrementality With Klaviyo", undated, https://ecommercefastlane.com/how-to-measure-email-sms-incrementality-with-klaviyo/)

Where do Klaviyo's benchmarks come from?

They are Klaviyo's own platform data, aggregated across its customers. Inside the product, Klaviyo compares you with a peer group of about 100 similar companies and shows the 25th, 50th and 75th percentile. (documented) (Sources: Klaviyo Help Center, "Understanding peer groups, benchmarks, and how Klaviyo uses your data", updated 15 Aug 2026, https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/360050180151; Klaviyo Help Center, "Percentiles in Klaviyo reference", updated 18 Aug 2026, https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/360049718852)

Its public 2026 figures (Klaviyo's data, attributed revenue): flows were 5.3% of email sends and produced nearly 41% of email revenue, with revenue per recipient (RPR, revenue divided by emails delivered) nearly 18 times campaigns. Flow click rates averaged 5.58% against 1.69% for campaigns, and nearly 48% of flow revenue came from new buyers against 16% for campaigns. The page leaves its customer count blank, so do not quote one for it. (Source: Klaviyo, "2026 email marketing benchmarks by industry", 2026, chart dates vary: open rate chart updated 11 Dec 2025, click rate chart 28 Jan 2026, https://www.klaviyo.com/products/email-marketing/benchmarks) Two charts on that page, headed email revenue per recipient and email order rate, look like SMS charts (our inference from their chart names), so do not read them as email figures.

By flow type (Klaviyo's data, more than 110,000 Klaviyo customers, average RPR): abandoned cart $6.77, welcome $5.75, browse abandonment $2.08, post-purchase $1.80, win-back $0.82. The same page says discounts in a cart flow can train shoppers to wait. (Source: Klaviyo blog, "2026 Abandoned Cart Benchmark Report", 2026, no date shown, https://www.klaviyo.com/blog/abandoned-cart-benchmarks)

How should I use the flow benchmarks by company size and order value?

Use the Help Center table, not the headline averages. It shows the 25th and 75th percentile RPR for abandoned cart, post-purchase and welcome flows, by annual revenue band ($1M or less, $1M to $5M, $5M to $20M) and by average order value (AOV). RPR is flow-attributed revenue divided by delivered emails, from thousands of Klaviyo customers. Find your revenue band and AOV row and read the two numbers as comparison values at the lower and upper ends of the range, not a guaranteed result for your flow. (Klaviyo's data) (Source: Klaviyo Help Center, "Benchmarks for flow emails reference", updated 8 Oct 2026, https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/360033669452)

Order value moves the figure a lot. For a $1M to $5M brand, the abandoned cart flow's range is $1.12 to $3.03 per recipient when AOV is $44 to $83, but $6.00 to $16.40 when AOV is $163 to $291. For welcome series in the same band and the $83 to $112 AOV row, the range is $0.51 to $3.93. Our reading: the $6.77 average cart figure sits above the 75th percentile in every row with AOV under $112, so high-order-value brands pull it up and it is not what a typical brand should expect. Some rows are missing from the $5M to $20M table, so do not fill them in.

Do flows really earn most of the email money?

Klaviyo says yes. Another platform vendor, Omnisend, found something similar: automations were 2% of email sends and 30% of revenue, earning 16 times more per send than campaigns (outside data: 150,000 brands, 27 billion emails sent in 2025, Omnisend's own attributed measure). (Source: Omnisend, "2026 ecommerce marketing report", Jan 2026, https://www.omnisend.com/2026-ecommerce-marketing-report) Both are platform vendors measuring their own customers. Klaviyo uses last-touch credit (above); no saved Omnisend page says which credit rule Omnisend uses. Our reading: flows go to people who have just done something, such as abandoning a cart, and were already likely to buy. A high RPR shows good timing. It does not show how many of those sales would have happened anyway. A holdout on one flow is the test.

Is it 5 times cheaper to keep a customer than to win one?

The sources we hold do not settle it. It is a widely repeated line, they disagree, and none measures the cost ratio.

Harvard Business Review's Amy Gallo wrote in 2014 that, "depending on which study you believe", winning a new customer costs five to 25 times more than keeping one, and she used that to argue for retention. Only her opening paragraph was available to us. It names no studies for the 5 to 25 line, though it goes on to cite Frederick Reichheld of Bain & Company for a different claim, that a 5% rise in retention lifts profits by 25% to 95%. (Source: HBR, "The Value of Keeping the Right Customers", 29 Oct 2014, https://hbr.org/2014/10/the-value-of-keeping-the-right-customers)

Two sources doubt it. Market researchers Jeffrey Hofmeyr and Butch Rice trace the line to US research in the late 1980s and argue it assumes kept customers spend more, pay full price and cost less to serve, which they say research does not show. That is argument with no new data. (Source: Ipsos, Hofmeyr and Rice, "Loyalty Myths" excerpt, 2003, https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/publication/2003-08/Ipsos_Loyalty_Myth_8_Excerpt.pdf) Byron Sharp, director of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, says marketers believed retention is cheaper "without any evidence". That is his opinion in a blog post. (Source: Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, Byron Sharp, "The Hole in the Leaky Bucket Theory", c. 2015, https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/the-hole-in-the-leaky-bucket-theory)

James Hurman said the 5 times line rests on "absolutely no evidence" in a talk at Klaviyo's own K:SYD event in May 2025, introducing a study Klaviyo paid for. It is a trade press report of one talk. The full report is behind a form and we have not seen it, so we build no verdict on it. (Sources: Mi3, 13 May 2025, https://www.mi-3.com.au/13-05-2025/its-big-myth-james-hurman-klaviyo-study-anz-online-retailers-17m-online-shoppers; Australian Marketing Institute, May 2025, https://ami.org.au/knowledge-hub/its-a-big-myth-james-hurman-klaviyo-study-of-anz-online-retailers-1-7m-shoppers-explodes-adage-that-its-5x-cheaper-to-retain-than-acquire-new-customers/)

Does keeping more customers make a brand grow faster?

This is a different question from cost, and the evidence is observational, so cause cannot be read off it.

Hurman's study, which Klaviyo commissioned, covers 1.7 million customers and $1.2 billion of spend at dozens of Australian and New Zealand online retailers, with data aggregated by two Klaviyo agency partners. Brands that kept fewer customers but raised what those customers spent grew more than 3 times faster than brands with higher retention and flat or declining spend. Retailers with loyalty programs kept more customers (about 30% against 20%) yet averaged 14% revenue growth against 48% for those without, over the study's two-year span. This is one study, vendor-commissioned, not randomised, and its method is not public. Our reading: a fast-growing brand keeps adding new buyers, which can make its retention rate look low, so the cause may run the other way. (Klaviyo's data) (Sources: Klaviyo blog, James Hurman, "Why Retaining More Customers Might Hurt Your Growth", 8 May 2025, https://www.klaviyo.com/blog/retention-paradox-james-hurman; B&T, "The $2 Billion Mistake Australian Retailers Could Be Making With Customer Retention", 2025, https://www.bandt.com.au/the-2-billion-mistake-australian-retailers-could-be-making-with-customer-retention/)

Who actually buys, and what does that mean for email?

Ehrenberg-Bass research finds that loyalty follows brand size. Smaller brands have fewer buyers and those buyers are a little less loyal, a pattern called Double Jeopardy. A 2017 paper tested it in 32 replications, some 35 years apart, and found no boundary condition (outside data, peer-reviewed, abstract only; not about email). (Source: Graham, Bennett, Franke, Henfrey and Nagy-Hamada, Australasian Marketing Journal 25(4), 2017, https://openresearch.lsbu.ac.uk/item/86wv9)

Sharp adds that a typical brand has a great many light buyers, so a brand with 5% yearly penetration can reach 10% over three years without growing, because many customers skip years (practitioner view from a researcher, citing his published work). A five-year panel of the top 20 brands in 10 consumer goods categories found that the share of one year's buyers who did not buy the brand in a later year rose from 57% in year 2 to 71.5% by year 5, while the brands held steady share (outside data, peer-reviewed). (Sources: Sharp, as above; "The Long-Term Erosion of Repeat-Purchase Loyalty", European Journal of Marketing accepted manuscript, 2018, https://openresearch.lsbu.ac.uk/download/be035468354820a271bdfca8904e321d049ba257163abe6468f7bd7cefd8088a/321658/Long%20Term%20Erosion%20of%20Repeat%20Purchase%20Loyalty%20EJM%20Proof.pdf)

What this means for email is our reading, since none of these studies is about email: email reaches people who have already given you an address, so it works on existing and known buyers. It can time their next purchase well. It does not find new buyers, which come from reaching people outside your list. Klaviyo's "48% of flow revenue from new buyers" fits this, because those buyers had already signed up. A 1997 Sharp paper, as summarised on a blog, found loyalty program members bought in line with market share, though it studied plastic reward cards, not email programs (secondary source). (Source: R-bloggers, Florian Teschner, Jan 2026, https://www.r-bloggers.com/2026/01/do-loyalty-programs-actually-create-loyalty/)

Do discounts in email work?

Two kinds of evidence point different ways.

Klaviyo and ProfitPeak's "Sugar High!" paper is described on Klaviyo's blog as an independent research paper produced in partnership with Klaviyo ("Klaviyo-commissioned" is the word used for the 2025 Hurman study, not this one), and it is behind a form, so we have only Hurman's Klaviyo blog post. Klaviyo's data on thousands of brands, Q1 2025 to Q1 2026: brands with low discount rates grew gross merchandise value 12% against 6% for deep discounters (11 or more promotions a year), and margins rose 8% against a fall of 11%. ProfitPeak's data on 176 Australian brands over 12 months: 88% of orders were discounted; 68% of discounted orders came from returning customers against 11% of full-price orders; revenue 2 to 4 weeks after a sale was 27% below baseline; 90% of revenue in sitewide sales came from bestsellers and core lines. These are two samples. Both are correlations, and our reading is that brands already in trouble may discount more. (Source: Klaviyo blog, James Hurman, "Your Discount Strategy Is Making You Poorer", 20 May 2026, https://www.klaviyo.com/blog/discount-pricing-strategies)

Sahni, Zou and Chintagunta ran randomised experiments, the strongest design here. Across 70 email experiments on one US ticket resale platform from July 2009 to Dec 2011, customers sent offers spent $3.03 more (37%) during the promotion than those who got none. Only about 10% of the extra spend came through redeemed offers, and spending rose on events not in the offer. Booth's summary reports $1.55 more per purchase in the following week, and Stanford's reports a 25% bump. Sahni notes a market with few sellers may not carry over to general retailers, and the data is old. Our reading: it shows contact can lift buying. It did not test discount email against no-discount email, so it neither backs nor refutes heavy discounting. (Outside data) (Sources: Chicago Booth Review, 11 Jun 2015, https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/just-how-effective-are-free-shipping-emails; Stanford GSB Insights, 21 Jan 2015, https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/navdeep-sahni-email-promotions-prospective-customers-really-do-work)

Does email return $36 for every $1?

That figure is the edge of a band. Litmus's State of Email 2025 survey of nearly 500 marketers found 35% saw $10 to $36 per $1, 30% saw $36 to $50, and 5% saw more than $50. The page does not say what the other 30% reported. 21% did not measure ROI, down from 36% in 2023, and only 41% used direct revenue attribution to email, so many answers inherit the attribution limit above. Its 45:1 figure is for "Retail, ecommerce, & consumer goods" and comes from a separate 2020 survey of over 2,000. These are self-reported by a company that sells email software. (Source: Litmus, "The ROI of Email Marketing", undated, https://www.litmus.com/blog/email-marketing-roi)

Where is the evidence fair to email?

The one randomised study (Sahni) found email lifted spending, including on products not in the offer. Clicks and orders are measurable where opens are not. Klaviyo and Omnisend both show flows converting better than one-off campaigns. Missing is a published holdout result for Klaviyo flows. A holdout costs a slice of revenue for a few weeks and turns a platform's number into your own.
