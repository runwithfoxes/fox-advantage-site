# Kite Insurance: tone of voice

> ⚠️ **Kite is a fictional brand, invented for this course.**
>
> Split out of `messaging-framework.md` on 26 July 2026, because it is the file the writer reaches
> for most often and it should not be a section inside a bigger document.

Kite does not speak. The customer speaks, and Kite arrives at the end.

**Whose voice.** First person, always. It is one person working themselves up about something
absurd that everyone has agreed to treat as normal. Not a narrator, not a brand talking about
itself, and never a cheerful team.

**Register.** Dry, weary, disbelieving. The sound of the only sane person in the room pointing at
the obvious. Never friendly, never corporate, never delighted.

**The shape.** Five beats, in this order.

1. **The reasonable expectation.** Something nobody could argue with. *"Same car. Same house. Same
   me."*
2. **The absurd reality.** The thing that happens anyway. *"But the price goes up."*
3. **The spiral, with specifics.** This is where it lives or dies. Real objects, real numbers, real
   places. A 61-page policy document, a Tuesday, a Ford Focus with 140,000 kilometres on it. Vague
   is not funny.
4. **The logic bomb.** Treat the obvious contract as sacred. *"That's the arrangement. I stay, and
   I pay more for it."*
5. **The flat landing.** Kite appears, understated, and does not celebrate. Competence delivered as
   mildly surprising news.

**The device.** *"What are you, a ___?"* One line, second person, naming the absurd job the
category has quietly handed the customer. A procurement department. A renewal calendar. It carries
the disbelief in a single line and it is the thing that makes this a voice rather than a set of
one-off jokes.

Use it sparingly and never twice in one piece. It is the most recognisable thing Kite says, and it
stops being recognisable if it is in everything.

**Never triumphant.** The endline collapses rather than lands. The joke is that doing the
straightforward thing is somehow remarkable, so the brand must never act as though it agrees.

**Never at the customer's expense.** The customer is the one who is right. Nobody is being told
they should have shopped around, or left it too late, or read the small print. The absurdity is the
system's, not theirs.

**Never explain the machinery.** How the automation works is not the joke and it is not the copy.
Pillar 3 says it shows you what it did, and that is as technical as the voice goes.

**Worked example, in Kite's world:**

> Same car. Same house. Same me. Same driveway, same forty minute commute, same nothing-happened.
> But the price goes up. What am I paying for, exactly? Did the Focus get faster overnight?
> Apparently I'm being charged for staying. That's the arrangement. I stay put, and it costs me
> extra.
>
> Kite. It shops around. You don't.

## Addendum, 26 July 2026: written posts are second person

⛔ The tone section above says *"Kite does not speak. The customer speaks, and Kite arrives at the
end."* That holds for radio, where a script label tells the listener a customer is talking.

**It breaks in a written social post.** Read cold there is no label, so first person reads as
nobody, and the reader cannot tell who is speaking or what is being sold. Four drafts died on this.

**In written posts, use second person: the brand narrating your situation back to you.** Every
other beat of the spec is unchanged. Worked examples: `segment-posts.md`.
