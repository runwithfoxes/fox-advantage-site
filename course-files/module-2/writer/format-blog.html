How to write a blog post

Add this file to your Claude project with the others. It is the same for every brand.

Nobody has to read a blog post. There is no inbox putting it in front of them and no ad budget holding them there. It gets read because the title earned it and the first paragraph paid for it.

Instructions for Claude

The title is most of the job

More people will see the title than will ever read the piece, so it carries more weight than any other line in it.

⭐ Write it last. You cannot name a piece properly until it exists and you know what it actually delivers. Then write five versions, pick one, and say which you picked and why.

  • Specific beats clever. "What automatic renewal actually costs you" beats "The future is now".
  • Promise, not topic. A topic tells them what it is about. A promise tells them what they get.
  • Sentence case. Title Case Reads As Template.
  • Never promise what the body cannot pay. If the title says three things, the body has three things.
  • If there is a real search term, put it near the front, but not at the cost of the line. A title that ranks and reads badly loses the reader you just paid to attract.

The blocks, in order

BlockWhat it has to do
TitleThe promise. One per piece.
StandfirstOne or two sentences under the title saying what they get and why it is worth the time. It does not repeat the title. This is the "should I read this" decision.
OpeningTwo or three sentences. The point arrives here. No throat-clearing, no scene-setting nobody asked for.
SubheadsEvery 200 to 300 words. Each one a real signpost, descriptive rather than cute. "How the renewal actually works", not "The plot thickens".
ParagraphsTwo to four sentences. One idea each. Two ideas means two paragraphs.
ListsOnly for three or more genuinely parallel things, each with substance. Never as decoration.
Pull quoteOptional, one at most, to surface the sharpest line in the piece.
One action at the endBenefit-led and specific. Never "learn more".

The skim test

Read back only the title, the standfirst and the subheads. Does that alone tell the whole argument?

If it does not, the subheads are not doing their job. Most readers will skim before they commit, and a piece that only makes sense when read in full will not get read in full.

Two habits that pass the test. Front-load every section, so the point of a paragraph is in its first sentence rather than its last. And leave white space, because crowded text reads cheap and goes unread.

Length

Set by what the piece is, never by a target.

WordsWhen
Short400 to 700A reaction, a recap, one finding explained
Standard700 to 1,100An explainer, a practical guide, a case study
Long1,100 to 1,500A point of view, an argument being built

Past about 1,500 words a business reader is mostly gone, and the piece starts accumulating the statistical patterns described in the slop rules. If the material genuinely needs more, it is two pieces rather than one long one.

⛔ Never pad to sound authoritative. Length is earned by having more to say.

Search, lightly

The order is always: real, specific and well written first. Findable second.

  • One search term if there is a natural one. Title, first paragraph, one subhead. That is enough. Stuffing is both a slop tell and a ranking penalty now.
  • The standfirst doubles as the description in search results. Around 150 characters saying what the piece delivers.
  • Descriptive subheads work twice, once for the skimmer and once for the search engine.
  • Link to the actual source when you cite something. Honest sourcing is good for both trust and ranking.
  • ⛔ Never invent an angle to chase a term, and never write a sentence whose only job is to carry a keyword.

If search and the writing ever pull against each other, the writing wins.

Before you show it

  • Title written last, five versions, the choice explained.
  • The standfirst earns the read and does not repeat the title.
  • The point is in the first paragraph, not the fourth.
  • Skim test passed. Title, standfirst and subheads carry the argument alone.
  • One idea per paragraph, lengths varied.
  • One action at the end, specific.
  • Slop rules run, both passes.
  • Claims ledger shown.
  • Scored against the messaging framework, honestly.
